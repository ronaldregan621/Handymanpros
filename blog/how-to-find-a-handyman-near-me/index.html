Searching "handyman near me" gets you a list, not an answer. You get a map with pins on it, profiles with star ratings that all sit between 4.6 and 4.9, and no way to tell which of those people will answer the phone, show up on the day they said, and charge what they quoted.

The Three Things That Actually Go Wrong

Before the checklist, it helps to know what you are filtering for. Complaints about handyman services cluster into three failures, and they have nothing to do with skill.

  1. The no show. The most common complaint by a wide margin. Someone quotes, agrees to a day, and then goes quiet. Often the job was too small to be worth their drive, and rather than say so they simply stopped responding.
  2. The moving price. An hourly quote that grows, or a flat quote that gets revised on the doorstep once the person sees the job. The second version is worse, because you are standing in your own hallway with no leverage and a stranger holding a drill.
  3. The wrong hardware. The quiet one. The work gets done, you pay, and it looks fine. Six months later the shelf pulls out of the wall or the television sags, because a plastic anchor was carrying a load it was never rated for.

Everything below is designed to catch one of those three before you commit.

The Vetting Checklist

1. Ask whether the price is flat or hourly, and what changes it

Hourly billing puts the person setting the pace on the paying side of the clock. If a quote is hourly, ask directly: what is the minimum, is there a trip charge, and what happens if the wall turns out to be plaster. Those three answers tell you the real range. A flat quote should come with the questions that justify it, which is a good sign rather than an annoyance.

2. Ask what they will do if there is no stud where the job needs one

This is the single best technical filter, because it is the situation that comes up constantly and it exposes whether someone has a method or a habit. A good answer names specific hardware: toggle style anchors with a stated load rating, or a mounting board spanning two studs. A bad answer is "we'll figure it out" or "the anchors I use hold anything."

3. Ask about insurance, and ask for the certificate

General liability insurance is what covers the repair if something goes wrong in your home. Someone genuinely insured will produce a certificate without hesitation, because they send them to building managers all the time. Hesitation here is the answer. If you live in an apartment building or a cooperative, you will likely need that certificate on file with management anyway before anyone is allowed to work.

4. Find out who is actually coming

Some booking platforms sell your job to whichever contractor claims it first, which is why the star rating you read may belong to a different person than the one at your door. Ask whether the person you are speaking to is doing the work. Neither answer is disqualifying, but you should know which one you are getting.

5. Ask what they will not do

This sounds backwards and it is the most revealing question on the list. Someone who works honestly will name limits immediately: no panel work, no new circuits, no gas or drain lines, no structural changes. Someone who says they can do everything is telling you they will attempt licensed work without a license, which leaves you with unpermitted work you have to disclose when you sell.

6. Confirm the arrival window and how you will be told about delays

The no show usually announces itself in the vagueness of the scheduling conversation. "Sometime Tuesday" is a worse sign than "Tuesday between 10 and 12, and we text if we are running late." You are testing for a system, not for politeness.

Ratings are a weak filter, and everybody knows it. Nearly every local service profile sits between 4.6 and 4.9 stars, so the rating carries almost no information. What does carry information is how recent the reviews are and whether they describe jobs like yours. Six recent reviews describing plaster wall installs beat two hundred old ones averaging 4.8.

What a Handy Person Can and Cannot Legally Do

The line matters and it is not obvious. In both New York and Connecticut, some work requires a licensed trade and a permit, regardless of how simple it looks.

Handyman scope. Mounting and anchoring, furniture and equipment assembly, hardware replacement, doors and locks, shelving, picture and mirror hanging, grab bars and handrails, smoke and carbon monoxide detector swaps, light bulbs and fixture replacements on existing wiring, weather stripping, patching, caulking, and small carpentry.

Licensed trade scope. New electrical circuits, service panel work, anything altering the wiring in the walls, gas lines, water supply and drain line modification, structural framing changes, and roofing beyond a minor repair. These require a licensed electrician, plumber, or contractor pulling a permit, and municipalities across Westchester, the Bronx, and Fairfield County all enforce this.

The practical risk of ignoring the line is not usually a catastrophe. It is a closing. Unpermitted work turns up during a sale or an insurance claim, and at that point you are paying a licensed trade to redo it correctly plus the permit you avoided in the first place.

How to Describe Your Job So the Quote Holds

Most price surprises are information failures rather than dishonesty. Give these details up front and the number you are quoted is the number you pay:

  • What the walls are. Drywall, plaster, tile, brick, or concrete. If you do not know, say the year the building went up. Anything before about 1960 is probably plaster.
  • Sizes and quantities. Television size in inches, the number of furniture pieces and their model names, how many detectors, ceiling height for bulb work.
  • Access. Which floor, whether there is an elevator, whether parking is difficult, whether building management requires an insurance certificate or restricts work hours.
  • Photos. One photo of the wall and one of the room does more than three paragraphs of description. It is the fastest way to a quote that does not move.
  • The whole list. Not just the biggest item. Which brings us to the last point.

Book the List, Not the Task

Every visit carries fixed overhead that has nothing to do with the work: the drive, the parking, the tools coming out of the van, the setup and the pack up. That cost exists whether you booked one item or seven.

So the most effective way to reduce what you spend is to stop booking things individually. Walk through your home with your phone and photograph everything that has been quietly bothering you. The television still on the floor. The box in the spare room. The chirping detector. The bulb nobody can reach. That is one appointment and one price, and it costs far less per item than five separate visits spread across a year.
